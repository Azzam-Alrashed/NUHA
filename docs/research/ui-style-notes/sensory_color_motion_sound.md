# Sensory-friendly visual and audio design for autistic, ADHD and learning-difficulty children (NUHA)

Scope: color, contrast, visual complexity, animation/motion, and sound in children's educational apps. Evidence labels used below:
- **[EMP]**: empirical finding, with sample, age and population noted
- **[META]**: systematic review or meta-analysis
- **[GUIDE]**: standard or official guideline
- **[OPINION]**: practitioner or advocacy opinion

Current as of October 2026. Searches were limited (about 15 tool calls), so some primary papers were read only as abstracts or summaries. This is flagged where it applies.

---

## 1. Color preferences and sensory sensitivity in autistic children: is a "calm/muted palette" evidence-based?

### Takeaway
The direct evidence on color preference and color reactivity in autism comes from a handful of small, unreplicated studies. They do lean the same way: highly saturated or high-luminance colors, such as bright yellow, may be aversive or overstimulating for some autistic children. Read across these studies, a muted palette is a reasonable precaution that matches guidelines. It is not proven to improve learning, and that claim is closer to well-motivated folk wisdom than to an established finding.

### Cited Findings
- **[EMP] Grandgeorge & Masataka (2016), *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1976.** The sample was 29 boys with ASD aged 4–17 and 38 age-matched typically developing (TD) boys, who ranked 6 colors (red, pink, yellow, brown, green, blue). ASD boys were significantly less likely than TD boys to prefer yellow and more likely to prefer green and brown. The authors attribute this to hypersensitivity, with yellow perceived as sensory-overloading. The study had boys only, a wide age range, and only 6 colors — [PMC5179595](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5179595); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01976/epub)
- In the Kyoto University press release, both groups ranked red and blue highest. It suggests yellow's high luminance may explain why it feels overstimulating. That luminance explanation is the authors' interpretation and was not tested — [Kyoto Univ. press release (JP)](https://www.kyoto-u.ac.jp/ja/research-news/2016-12-26-1)
- **Replication status:** I searched for an independent replication or a 2020–2023 follow-up of the yellow-aversion finding and found none. Treat it as a single preliminary study — [PMC5179595](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5179595/)
- **[EMP] Eye-tracking by hue, luminance and saturation (2025, *J Autism Dev Disord*, DOI 10.1007/s10803-025-07100-7).** The sample was 34 ASD children and 40 TD peers aged 6–16, viewing picture sets that varied hue, luminance and saturation independently. The ASD group showed about 29% less total fixation time, about 19% fewer fixations and about 42% fewer saccades. Highly saturated images amplified the group differences; hue and luminance produced parallel but smaller effects. The effects were independent of pupil size. This is a single study, and I read it via abstract and a JoVE machine summary, not the full text — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-025-07100-7); [JoVE summary](https://visualize.jove.com/41184580)
  - This shows that saturation changes how autistic children look at images (less exploration). It does not show discomfort or worse learning; reduced looking could be read either way.
- **[EMP] Color perception differs, but not always as "hypersensitivity."** Franklin et al. (2010, *Developmental Science*) tested children with high-functioning autism against age and non-verbal-ability matched TD children, using the Farnsworth-Munsell 100-hue test plus a threshold task. The autism group made more errors and had higher chromatic thresholds, meaning *reduced* chromatic discrimination. On the hue test they performed like TD children about 3 years younger. Sample sizes were small — [Sussex repository](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/14099/); [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~kjameson/FranklinAUTISMCOLOR.PDF)
- **[EMP]** Franklin et al. (2008, *JADD*): children with autism were less accurate than TD children at detecting chromatic targets on chromatic backgrounds. Categorical color perception did not differ — [Surrey repository](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/bookChapter/Colour-and-autism-spectrum-disorders/99516314902346)
- **[EMP]** A 2017 study using the Cambridge Colour Test found color-vision losses in 6 of 20 autistic individuals (30%). The mechanism (retinal, cortical or both) is unknown — [Frontiers in Psychology 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01127/pdf)
- **[META/REVIEW]** A 2026 systematic review (29 studies, 2015–2025) of light and sound hypersensitivity in autism reports that 70–90% of autistic individuals have sensory hypersensitivity difficulties. It flags uneven evidence by age and gender, and was listed as provisionally accepted when retrieved — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1771956/full)
- **[GUIDE/OPINION]** The GOV.UK Home Office autism poster (Karwai Pun, 2016) says: "Do use simple colours" and "Don't use bright contrasting colours." It is presented as general guidance, not prescriptive rules, and is based on practitioner and user research rather than controlled experiments — [GOV.UK accessibility blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02/dos-and-donts-on-designing-for-accessibility/); [St Andrews summary](https://digitalcommunications.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/2019/07/08/designing-for-users-on-the-autistic-spectrum/)
- A guide derived from the poster says some users are sensitive to colours, which can cause anxiety or pain — [ideas.org.au](https://www.ideas.org.au/facts/designing-for-users-on-the-autistic-spectrum.html)

### Inferences
- NUHA's low-saturation teal/sand palette on warm off-white matches the direction of the weak evidence: avoid high saturation and high-luminance yellow as large fields, and prefer green/blue-ish hues. Nothing found shows it *improves* learning outcomes. It is best framed as harm reduction and comfort.
- Sand and yellow-adjacent hues: Grandgeorge's yellow was a saturated primary, while sand is low-saturation and is not the same stimulus. There is no evidence that muted sand is aversive. Keep saturated yellow (stars, reward bursts) small or optional.
- Some autistic children have *reduced* chromatic discrimination (Franklin 2010). Very subtle hue-only distinctions in a muted palette could be hard to tell apart. Information must not rely on hue alone; use luminance contrast, shape, icons and position. This also covers the roughly 8% of Saudi/Arab boys likely to have red-green color-vision deficiency, a general population figure I did not verify in this search.
- All color studies used children aged 4–17. None isolates 2–6-year-olds, and none used Arab/Saudi samples. Cultural color associations were not studied.

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled study was found comparing muted and saturated UI palettes on learning, engagement or distress in autistic children using apps.
- No independent replication of the yellow-aversion finding was found.
- No color-preference data for girls with ASD, for ADHD, or for children with learning difficulties.
- No data for Saudi/Gulf populations.

## 2. Visual clutter and complexity, and attention in ADHD and autism

### Takeaway
The best-known experiment, Fisher et al. 2014 (n=24 TD kindergarteners), found that heavily decorated surroundings cut attention and learning. Multimedia meta-analysis in young children separates two things: animation and sound that are *congruent* with the content help, while extraneous interactive extras hurt. Autism eye-tracking work suggests complex or unpredictable stimuli are harder to process. The evidence for sparse, purposeful screens is moderate. The ADHD-specific and autism-specific app evidence is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **[EMP] Fisher, Godwin & Seltman (2014), *Psychological Science*, DOI 10.1177/0956797614533801.** 24 kindergarteners, in a within-subjects design, had six science lessons in a lab classroom (3 decorated, 3 sparse). In the decorated room they were more distracted, spent more time off task and learned less: 42% test accuracy versus 55% in the sparse room — [APS release](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/heavily-decorated-classrooms-disrupt-attention-and-learning-in-young-children.html); [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2014/may/may27_decoratedclassrooms.html)
  - The sample was typically developing children, small, in a lab setting. Fisher herself advised teachers against stripping classrooms bare and called for real-classroom research — [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2014/may/may27_decoratedclassrooms.html)
  - **Critique:** Imuta & Scarf (2014) argued the effect may reflect *novelty* rather than irrelevance, and that relevant decorations can act as memory cues — [Frontiers commentary, PMC4274873](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4274873/)
- **[META] Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015), *Review of Educational Research* 85(4):698–739.** 43 studies, 2,147 children (young, mostly preschool/kindergarten). Technology-enhanced storybooks gave a small benefit for comprehension (g+=0.17) and expressive vocabulary (g+=0.20). *Multimedia* features (animated pictures, music, sound effects congruent with the story) helped. *Interactive* features (hotspots, games, dictionaries) distracted. Interactive features were particularly detrimental for children from less stimulating home environments — [Open Dev Ed](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/9LS8BP3N); [SAGE DOI](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)
- **[REVIEW]** Enhanced e-book features not linked to story content can distract children aged 3–5 — [ASHA Leader summary](https://leader.pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/leader.RIB4.22012017.16)
- **[EMP] Arora et al. (2022), *JADD*.** Children aged 7–15. Autistic features were associated with longer looking at repeating stimuli and shorter looking at changing stimuli, but only for *more complex* stimuli. This points to difficulty processing complex or unpredictable information — [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/5412076); [UEA manuscript](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/79471/1/Accepted_Manuscript.pdf)
- **[EMP]** An effective literacy app gave 4–5-year-olds gains after 20 minutes a day for ten weeks. Its designers deliberately excluded visual and sound effects unrelated to the vocabulary, to limit distraction — [ScienceAlert summary](https://www.sciencealert.com/here-are-the-kids-educational-apps-that-actually-work-according-to-science)
- **[OPINION/expert review]** Researchers rating top children's apps advise holding attention on the lesson rather than using overwhelming sound effects, flash ads and gimmicky rewards — [Penn State news](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/top-educational-apps-children-might-not-be-beneficial-promised)
- **[GUIDE]** W3C COGA ("Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities", W3C Group Note, 29 April 2021):
  - Objective 5: "Avoid distracting the user from their tasks."
  - Patterns 4.6.1 Limit Interruptions, 4.6.3 Avoid Too Much Content, and 4.4.10 Use White Spacing.
  - User story 3.5.1: users need to avoid distraction and be able to turn distractions off easily.
  - User story 3.3.1: users need to distinguish content from background distractions.
  - [W3C TR coga-usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[GUIDE]** The GOV.UK autism poster says "build simple and consistent layouts" and "Don't build complex and cluttered layouts" — [GOV.UK blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02/dos-and-donts-on-designing-for-accessibility/)
- **[GUIDE]** BSI PAS 6463:2022 ("Design for the mind – Neurodiversity and the built environment") covers lighting, acoustics, décor, layout and pattern for neurodivergent users. It applies to buildings, not software, and its specific recommendations are behind a paywall and were not retrieved — [BSI](https://bsigroup.com/ar-AE/1/pas-6463); [RIBA Journal](https://www.ribaj.com/spec/principles-for-neurodiverse-design-british-standard-autism-dyslexia-dispraxia-dementia/)

### Inferences
- For NUHA: use one primary task per screen, no decorative background illustrations or patterns behind task content, and generous white space. Any decoration should be content-related; this addresses the novelty and relevance critique.
- Keep scene backgrounds consistent across sessions so they lose novelty. This follows the Imuta & Scarf argument and is not tested in apps.
- Prefer congruent narration plus illustration (helps, per Takacs 2015) over tappable hotspots or extras that are not part of the learning task (distract).
- Children who struggle most (low home stimulation, and by plausible extension children with ADHD) are likely the most hurt by extraneous interactivity. This extension to ADHD is not directly tested.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found on visual clutter in *apps* specifically for ADHD or autistic preschoolers.
- "Visual crowding" in the narrow psychophysics sense (letter crowding in dyslexia) was not searched. It is relevant for Arabic letter spacing; see the typography research.
- Fisher 2014 has not been replicated at scale with neurodivergent children, as far as I found.

## 3. Contrast: WCAG 2.2 levels, low vision in children, and reconciling calm palettes with contrast

### Takeaway
WCAG 2.2 AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text, and 3:1 for UI components and meaningful graphics. "Calm" and "sufficient contrast" are compatible: lower *saturation* is what calms, while *luminance* contrast between foreground and background is what makes things legible. The GOV.UK poster itself acknowledges the tension and recommends user testing.

### Cited Findings
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), Level AA:** text and images of text need at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (18pt, or 14pt bold) — [W3C Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced), Level AAA:** 7:1 for normal text and 4.5:1 for large text — [W3C Understanding 1.4.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-enhanced.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast, Level AA:** UI component boundaries, states and graphical objects needed to understand content need 3:1 against adjacent colors — [W3C Understanding 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1 Use of Color:** color must not be the only visual means of conveying information — [W3C Understanding 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.11 / 2.4.13 Focus Appearance (new in 2.2):** visible focus indicators. These matter for stylus and switch users — [W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- **[GUIDE]** The GOV.UK blog says bright contrast was advised for some users, such as those with low vision, while some users on the autistic spectrum would prefer otherwise. It recommends testing with users where advice seems contradictory — [GOV.UK blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02/dos-and-donts-on-designing-for-accessibility/)
- **[GUIDE]** COGA notes that some people with cognitive impairments "may also require higher contrast" because of co-occurring needs — [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[EMP]** Reduced chromatic discrimination in autistic children (Franklin 2010) means hue differences alone are unreliable. Luminance contrast is the safer channel — [Sussex](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/14099/)

### Inferences
- Reconciliation strategy for NUHA:
  - Keep large surfaces low-saturation (sand and off-white backgrounds, muted teal).
  - Make text and key interactive affordances dark and desaturated, such as deep teal or near-black warm brown on off-white, so they pass 4.5:1 or ideally 7:1.
  - "Avoid bright contrasting colours" (GOV.UK) is best read as avoiding *saturated complementary color clashes* such as red on green or yellow on blue. It is not a reason to lower luminance contrast below WCAG.
  - Avoid pure #000 on pure #FFF glare. A warm off-white with dark text is a common compromise; this is practitioner convention, not evidence.
- Interactive targets (buttons, tiles) need a 3:1 boundary against the background. Light teal on sand will often fail this, so NUHA's palette should be audited with a contrast checker.
- A "high-contrast" mode should be offered for low-vision children and children with co-occurring visual impairment, which is common in rehab-center populations. This follows from guidance, not from a NUHA-specific study.
- Large text matters for young children: they learn to read at larger sizes, so large-text thresholds (3:1) will often apply. Still, aim for 4.5:1 or higher.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found on contrast preferences or thresholds specifically for autistic or ADHD children using screens.
- The prevalence of pediatric low vision in Saudi Arabia was not searched.
- WCAG 2.x contrast math (the relative luminance formula) is known to be imperfect. APCA, the method proposed for WCAG 3, was not researched here.

## 4. Animation and motion: distraction, reduced motion, vestibular sensitivity, flashing, feedback

### Takeaway
The firm rules are WCAG's: no content that flashes more than 3 times per second; pause/stop/hide for auto-moving content longer than 5 seconds; and (AAA) the ability to disable motion triggered by interaction. Android exposes "Remove animations," which Compose (1.2+) respects automatically. Learning-related evidence suggests animation that is *congruent* with content helps young children, while decorative or competing motion distracts. I found no controlled study isolating correct/incorrect feedback animation in preschool apps.

### Cited Findings
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold (A):** no content flashes more than 3 times in any one-second period, unless below the general and red flash thresholds — [W3C Understanding 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.2 Three Flashes (AAA):** no flashing more than 3 times per second, with no threshold exception — [W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA):** users can disable motion animation triggered by interaction unless it is essential. The rationale is vestibular disorders, where motion can cause dizziness or nausea — [W3C Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A):** moving, blinking or scrolling content that starts automatically, lasts more than 5 seconds and is shown alongside other content must be pausable, stoppable or hideable — [W3C Understanding 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
- **[GUIDE]** COGA patterns include 4.5.1 Ensure Controls and Content Do Not Move Unexpectedly and 4.9.1 Let Users Control When the Content Moves or Changes. Scenario 6.2.2 covers changing color schemes, flashing, blinking and autoplaying video or music — [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[GUIDE]** W3C notes that user controls to pause, stop or hide all moving content are currently missing from the WCAG 3 draft, and calls distraction a "sleeper topic" (COGA minutes, 2026) — [COGA minutes 25 June 2026](https://www.w3.org/2026/06/25-coga-minutes.html); [15 June 2026](https://www.w3.org/2026/06/15-coga-minutes.html)
- **[PLATFORM]** Android's "Remove animations" setting sets `Settings.Global.ANIMATOR_DURATION_SCALE` to 0. Jetpack Compose 1.2+ respects it automatically for its animation APIs. Views, older Compose, custom animations and third-party components need a manual check:
  - The `basicMarquee` modifier was reported to ignore the setting.
  - Wear Compose exposes `LocalReduceMotion`.
  - Some Samsung battery-saver modes also set the scale to 0, which stops even infinite animations.
  - [Inclusive Android Apps #8](https://buttondown.com/inclusive-android-apps/archive/inclusive-android-apps-8-the-problem-of-animations/); [ProAndroidDev basicMarquee](https://proandroiddev.com/making-basicmarquee-modifier-more-accessible-8b3737307dee); [composables.com LocalReduceMotion](https://composables.com/jetpack-compose/androidx.wear.compose/compose-foundation/properties/LocalReduceMotion); [Kotlin Slack](https://slack-chats.kotlinlang.org/t/5048569/just-got-some-reports-that-animations-no-more-runs-on-some-d?t=v2)
  - Version details were not verified against official release notes.
- **[META]** Animated pictures congruent with the story improved young children's story comprehension, while competing interactive extras distracted (Takacs et al. 2015, 43 studies, n=2,147) — [Open Dev Ed](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/9LS8BP3N)
- **[EMP, weak]** A 2026 pilot (Suda et al.) reported that ASD children fixated longer on repetitive than on random movements, while TD children showed no preference. I found it only via a secondary summary site, so it is unverified — [Behaviorist Book Club summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/relative-complexity-in-repetitive-structure-and-visual-preference-for/)
- **[EMP, weak]** A math-app pilot found that textual, icon and emoticon feedback types did not affect children's actual or perceived performance — [Arif SMC 2017](https://asarif.com/pub/Arif_SMC2017_Correctness_Feedback.pdf)
- **[EMP]** A survey of preschool math apps found 87% gave correctness-only feedback (right or wrong) — [Arif SMC 2017](https://asarif.com/pub/Arif_SMC2017_Correctness_Feedback.pdf)

### Inferences
- NUHA defaults:
  - No flashing at all; this beats 2.3.1 and meets 2.3.2.
  - No ambient or looping motion on task screens.
  - Short, slow transitions, with no parallax, zoom or spin, which are vestibular triggers.
  - Reward animations brief (about 1 second), small in screen area, and not using saturated full-screen bursts.
  - An in-app "reduce motion" toggle that defaults to the OS setting, because caregivers may not know the OS setting exists.
- Predictable, repetitive motion may be more comfortable for autistic children than random motion (weak evidence). Feedback animations should be consistent and identical every time, not randomized "surprise" effects.
- Children with ADHD are likely more vulnerable to motion competing with task content (extension of Takacs and Pelham; see section 5). Motion should always point *at* the learning target, for example highlighting the correct answer, rather than decorate.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found isolating correct/incorrect animated feedback in apps for autistic, ADHD or learning-difficulty children.
- No prevalence data were found on vestibular or motion sensitivity in autistic children specifically.
- No study was found on whether reward animations motivate or overstimulate young autistic children. This belongs in the reinforcement and ABA literature, which was not searched here.

## 5. Sound: background music, auditory hypersensitivity, voice prompts, feedback sounds, user control

### Takeaway
Sound sensitivity is common in autism: hyperacusis is reported in roughly 18–40% of autistic children, and broader sensory hypersensitivity in up to 70–90% of autistic individuals. So default audio should be gentle, and every sound category should be independently controllable. Background noise and music show a small *benefit* for ADHD children but *harm* non-ADHD peers (meta-analysis). This makes a single default impossible, and supports background audio that is off by default and optional. Voice narration congruent with content is useful for pre-readers.

### Cited Findings
- **[REVIEW]** Hyperacusis (increased sensitivity or decreased tolerance to sound) affects nearly 18–40% of children with autism, citing studies from 1999–2021. Bilateral hearing loss or deafness appears in about 3.5% — [Springer J Neural Transm 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00702-023-02595-9)
- **[REVIEW]** 70–90% of autistic individuals report sensory hypersensitivity difficulties, from a systematic review of 29 studies, 2015–2025 — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1771956/full)
- **[META] Nigg et al. (2024), *JAACAP*, DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2023.12.014, preregistered PROSPERO CRD42023393992.**
  - 13 randomized lab studies, 335 children and young adults with ADHD or ADHD symptoms.
  - White or pink noise gave a small significant benefit on attention and executive tasks (g=0.249).
  - In non-ADHD comparison groups it had a *negative* effect (g=−0.212, 95% CI −0.355 to −0.069).
  - Heterogeneity was low and no publication bias was found. No brown-noise studies qualified.
  - [PMC11283987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11283987/); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/does-pink-noise-really-help-you-focus)
- **[EMP]** Söderlund's Moderate Brain Arousal model holds that the optimal noise level is individual: noise helps poor attenders but hinders super-attentive children (Helps et al. 2014, *PLoS ONE*). Benefits applied to inattention, not hyperactivity — [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/375557); [CHADD](https://chadd.org/adhd-weekly/could-white-noise-help-you-stay-on-task/)
- **[EMP] Pelham et al. (2011), *J Abnormal Child Psychology*.** In classroom studies of boys with ADHD, video distracted all children, especially those with ADHD. Music did not hurt most children and improved productivity for some with ADHD — [GoodTherapy summary](https://www.goodtherapy.org/blog/do-boys-with-adhd-focus-better-with-music-1215111)
- **[EMP] Mendes et al. (2024), *Interactive J Med Res*.** 76 boys (34 ADHD, 42 TD). Music produced no reaction-time effects but fewer errors compared with silence. The review notes heterogeneous methods make it hard to draw conclusions — [i-jmr.org](https://www.i-jmr.org/2024/1/e53869)
- **[EMP, poster]** Madjar et al. (2020): background music improved reading comprehension only for ADHD students and may distract most others — [Carleton PDF summary](https://carleton.ca/mental-health/wp-content/uploads/background-music-for-adhd.pdf)
- **[META]** Congruent music and sound effects in storybooks aided young children's comprehension (Takacs et al. 2015) — [Open Dev Ed](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/9LS8BP3N)
- **[OPINION/REVIEW]** For pre-readers, audible cues (bells) or visual cues (a happy face) work better than text feedback — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/how-to-choose-educational-apps-for-pre-school-children-153575)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.2 Audio Control (A):** if audio plays automatically for more than 3 seconds, provide a way to pause or stop it, or to control its volume independently of system volume — [W3C Understanding 1.4.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/audio-control.html)
- **[GUIDE] WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.7 Low or No Background Audio (AAA):** prerecorded speech should have no background sound, or background sound at least 20 dB quieter than the speech, or sound that can be turned off — [W3C Understanding 1.4.7](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/low-or-no-background-audio.html)
- **[GUIDE]** COGA scenario 6.2.2 addresses automatically playing music. User stories ask that support be "in my control" and that users can turn distractions off easily — [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)

### Inferences
- NUHA audio defaults:
  - **Voice prompts on by default.** Arabic narration is essential for pre-readers. Use a calm, warm, moderate-pace voice.
  - **Background music off by default**, with an optional setting.
  - **Feedback sounds** soft, short and low-pitched. No harsh buzzer for "incorrect"; use a neutral sound or silence plus a re-prompt.
  - **Separate volume or toggles** for voice, effects and music in a caregiver-gated settings area.
  - Meet the WCAG 1.4.7 spirit: any music under narration at least 20 dB quieter, or off.
- The ADHD-benefit/non-ADHD-harm pattern argues for an optional "focus sound" (pink noise or soft music) as a per-child setting, never a global default. In resource rooms and rehab centers, where several children share a space, headphones or off-by-default matter, because noise harms non-ADHD peers. That last point is an extension of the BPS commentary.
- Since 18–40% of autistic children may have hyperacusis, sudden loud onsets (celebration fanfares) are a risk. Use volume-normalized, soft-attack sound assets.

### Gaps
- No study was found on background music specifically for autistic children during app learning.
- No study was found comparing harsh and gentle "incorrect" sounds, or sound and silent feedback, for young autistic children.
- No evidence was found on Arabic voice characteristics (gender, pace, dialect versus MSA) for this population. That is a separate research thread.
- Noise studies mostly involve school-age children and young adults, not 2–6-year-olds.

## 6. Official guidelines summary (COGA, GOV.UK, NAS, platform)

### Takeaway
Guidelines converge on simple colors, simple and consistent layouts, no unexpected motion, no flashing, user control over audio and motion, and support for personalization. They rest on expert consensus and user research rather than controlled trials. GOV.UK explicitly frames its poster as non-prescriptive and recommends testing with users.

### Cited Findings
- **[GUIDE] W3C COGA (Group Note, 29 April 2021):** supplemental to WCAG and not required for conformance. Its objectives include Objective 5 "Avoid distracting the user from their tasks" and Objective 8 "Support personalization when you can." Relevant patterns:
  - 4.4.10 Use White Spacing
  - 4.4.11 Ensure Foreground Content is not Obscured by Background
  - 4.5.1 Ensure Controls and Content Do Not Move Unexpectedly
  - 4.6.1 Limit Interruptions
  - 4.6.3 Avoid Too Much Content
  - 4.9.1 Let Users Control When the Content Moves or Changes
  - 4.9.3 Support Simplification
  - 4.9.4 Support a Personalized and Familiar Interface
  - [W3C TR coga-usable](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/); [2021 Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/2021/NOTE-coga-usable-20210429/)
- **[GUIDE] GOV.UK / Home Office autism poster (2016, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0).**
  - Do: use simple colours, plain English, short sentences and bullets, descriptive buttons, and simple consistent layouts.
  - Don't: use bright contrasting colours, figures of speech or idioms, walls of text, vague buttons, or complex cluttered layouts.
  - [GOV.UK blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02/dos-and-donts-on-designing-for-accessibility/); [poster set PDF](https://www.nfpw.org/assets/docs/2025/accessibility-posters-set.pdf)
- **[GUIDE] BSI PAS 6463:2022:** a neurodiversity guide for the built environment (lighting, acoustics, décor, layout). It is not a British Standard and is not software-specific — [BSI](https://bsigroup.com/ar-AE/1/pas-6463)
- **[PLATFORM]** Android "Remove animations" maps to `ANIMATOR_DURATION_SCALE`=0 (see section 4) — [Inclusive Android Apps](https://buttondown.com/inclusive-android-apps/archive/inclusive-android-apps-8-the-problem-of-animations/)

### Inferences
- NUHA's current calm palette and the minimal-clutter direction are fully consistent with every guideline found. The biggest guideline gaps to address are: user control over motion and sound, personalization, and verified contrast.

### Gaps
- National Autistic Society (UK) digital or website design guidance was not retrieved in this session.
- Apple's Reduce Motion and Human Interface Guidelines, and Google's Material accessibility motion guidance, were not fetched directly. Only Android developer-community sources were used.
- No Saudi or Gulf official guidance (for example from the Saudi Authority for Persons with Disabilities, or the digital-government accessibility standards) was searched.

## 7. Individual differences: hypo- vs hyper-sensitivity, and the case for personalization

### Takeaway
Large cluster and latent-profile studies consistently find 3–5 distinct sensory subtypes in autistic children, including hyper-responsive, hypo-responsive and sensory-seeking profiles. These often co-occur within one person and change with age. Noise research in ADHD shows the same stimulus helps some children and harms others. Together this is the strongest evidence-based argument in this review: use a calm default plus caregiver-adjustable settings, rather than one "correct" palette and soundscape.

### Cited Findings
- **[EMP]** A K-means cluster analysis of Short Sensory Profile data from 599 autistic people aged 1–21 found 5 sensory phenotypes, including an under-responsive/sensation-seeking profile. Adaptive behavior and autism, ADHD and OCD traits differed across profiles — [PMC8507349](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8507349/)
- **[EMP]** Ausderau and colleagues' subtyping uses four factors: hyporesponsiveness, hyperresponsiveness, sensory interests/repetitions/seeking, and enhanced perception. A latent profile transition analysis covered a large national sample of autistic children aged 2–12, so it includes young children — [Autism CRC report](https://www.autismcrc.com.au/sites/default/files/reports/1.075RU_Sensory-subtypes_Final_Report.pdf)
- **[EMP]** Subtyping models give 3–5 subtypes depending on measure and cohort — [INSAR 2017 abstract](https://insar.confex.com/imfar/2017/webprogram/Paper26037.html)
- **[EMP]** In adults, a high-frequency subgroup (n=17) showed hyper- and hypo-reactivity co-occurring — [mijn.bsl.nl](https://mijn.bsl.nl/sensory-clusters-of-adults-with-and-without-autism-spectrum-cond/11236216)
- **[EMP]** A study of 3,868 autistic youth examined whether HYPER, HYPO and SEEK form general factors across sensory modalities — [Reading CentAUR PMC10464466](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/113180/1/PMC10464466.pdf)
- **[EMP]** Sensory modulation subtypes vary by age and sex — [UWA repository](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/patterns-of-sensory-modulation-by-age-and-sex-in-young-people-on-/)
- **[META]** The same white or pink noise helps ADHD participants (g=+0.25) and harms non-ADHD participants (g=−0.21) — [PMC11283987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11283987/)
- **[EMP]** In the Moderate Brain Arousal model, the optimal stimulation level is individual — [CHADD](https://chadd.org/adhd-weekly/could-white-noise-help-you-stay-on-task/)
- **[GUIDE]** COGA Objective 8 says "Support personalization when you can," with pattern 4.9.4 Support a Personalized and Familiar Interface — [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)
- **[GUIDE]** GOV.UK says that where advice conflicts (high contrast for low vision versus softer contrast for some autistic users), designers should test with users — [GOV.UK blog](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2016/09/02/dos-and-donts-on-designing-for-accessibility/)

### Inferences
- Recommended NUHA personalization set, stored per child profile and set by a caregiver or therapist:
  1. Theme: calm default, high-contrast, and possibly an "extra-muted" low-stimulation variant.
  2. Motion: full, reduced (default follows OS) or none.
  3. Audio: separate voice, effects and music volumes, with music off by default and optional focus noise.
  4. Reward intensity: minimal (static check) to standard (short animation and sound).
  5. Option to hide decorative characters or illustrations.
- Hypo-responsive or sensory-seeking children may disengage with an overly muted, silent UI. A "standard" stimulation level should exist, but stay within WCAG flash and motion limits. This is an inference; no app study tested it.
- Defaults should be the calm, low-stimulation end. Hyper-responsive children are harmed more by over-stimulation than hypo-responsive children are by under-stimulation, which is risk-asymmetry reasoning rather than tested evidence. Rehab and resource-room staff can raise stimulation per child.
- Age: the Ausderau data cover ages 2–12, and the 599-person cluster study covers ages 1–21. Sensory-subtype heterogeneity is documented in young children, so it applies to both of NUHA's candidate age ranges.

### Gaps
- No study was found testing personalization settings themselves (for example caregiver-adjustable themes) in children's learning apps for autism or ADHD outcomes.
- No data were found on how stable a child's sensory profile is over weeks or months. That determines how often settings should be revisited; the age-related subtype transitions in Ausderau's work suggest it changes.
- Sensory profiles in children with learning difficulties without autism or ADHD were not covered by any source found.
