# Illustration/Character Style and Feedback/Reward Design for Children's Educational Apps (Autism, ADHD, Learning Difficulties) — notes for NUHA

Evidence labels used throughout: **[EMP]** = empirical finding (sample/age/population given where found); **[META]** = meta-analysis/systematic review; **[GUIDE]** = practitioner/clinical guideline or framework; **[OPINION]** = design write-up, vendor claim, student project, or author speculation. **[WEAK]** flags small-n, unverified-from-primary, or conference-abstract-only evidence. Research date: October 2026. Several numbers came from abstracts or secondary summaries, not full texts; these are marked.

---

## 1. Illustration fidelity: photos vs cartoon vs simplified/flat (emotion recognition, generalization, object recognition)

### Takeaway
Autistic children appear to process cartoon faces more typically than real faces (cartoons may be an easier entry point), but emotion skills trained on screen generalize poorly to real faces/real life unless training deliberately includes real photos and varied exemplars. For object recognition and transfer in very young children (under ~3), more realistic images (photos) transfer to real objects better than cartoons. Recommendation direction for NUHA: a graded fidelity ladder (simplified cartoon -> stylized-realistic -> real photos of diverse Saudi/Arab faces) for the emotion activity, and realistic-leaning (not abstract-icon) illustration for object/environment matching, especially if the 2–6 age band is chosen.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Rosset et al. (2008): 20 children with ASD vs two typically developing (TD) groups matched on chronological and mental age; processed emotional expressions of real faces, human cartoon faces and non-human cartoon faces. ASD children were as accurate as controls, but their processing strategies differed by face type. — [BSL abstract record](https://mijn.bsl.nl/typical-emotion-processing-for-cartoon-but-not-for-real-faces-in/547186); [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/typical-emotion-processing-for-cartoon-but-not-for-real-faces-in-child/)
  - [WEAK] Secondary summary claims ASD children used holistic (whole-face) processing for cartoons like TD peers, but switched to local/part-based processing for real faces. Verify against full paper. — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/typical-emotion-processing-for-cartoon-but-not-for-real-faces-in-child/)
- [EMP][WEAK, conference abstract] Same group (2012), affective priming with 26 children with high-functioning autism: TD children showed a priming effect only with negative *real* faces, whereas the ASD group showed priming only with negative *cartoon* faces — suggesting cartoons carry emotional meaning more automatically for autistic children. — [INSAR/IMFAR 2012 abstract](https://insar.confex.com/imfar/2012/webprogram/Paper10520.html)
- [EMP] Adults with high vs low autistic traits ("Reading the mind in cartoon eyes"): compared human vs cartoon emotion recognition; reported via secondary source that groups did not differ for human photos and autistic participants outperformed on cartoon versions. Adult/trait sample, so only indirect for children. — [PMC9136470](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9136470/); [secondary report](https://nevadaautism.com/autistic-people-outperform-neurotypicals-in-a-cartoon-version-of-an-emotion-recognition-task/)
- [EMP] Golan et al. (2010), *The Transporters* (animated vehicles with real human faces grafted on): 20 children with autism, aged 4–7, watched daily for 4 weeks; vs ASC and TD control groups. Intervention group improved significantly more than clinical controls on all three levels of generalization tested, reaching TD-level performance at follow-up. Authors note need to test lower-functioning children. — [JADD 2010, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-009-0862-9); [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/enhancing-emotion-recognition-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-co/)
  - Design relevance: the hybrid design (systemizing-friendly, predictable mechanical characters + real human faces) was deliberate; Baron-Cohen et al. argued the vehicles' mechanical nature is a key reason for gains. — [Bath portal (Baron-Cohen, Golan & Ashwin 2009)](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/can-emotion-recognition-be-taught-to-children-with-autism-spectru/)
  - Episode length reported inconsistently (5 vs 15 min) across secondary sources. — [OAR blog](https://researchautism.org/blog/a-closer-look-at-2010-oar-funded-research/)
- [EMP][WEAK, conference abstract] Gev, Rosenan & Golan (2016) note prior emotion-recognition interventions for ASD often generalized and maintained poorly, and added a parent guide to support generalization. — [IMFAR 2016 abstract](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2016/webprogram/Paper21850.html)
- [META] Meta-analysis of RCTs of facial emotion training in ASD (Zhao/Zhang et al., ~595 participants): robust improvement in emotion recognition vs controls; but the few studies with follow-ups/social-skill measures found gains not maintained and no evidence of general improvement in social skills. — [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Weihua-Zhao-12/publication/353362177_Facial_emotion_training_as_an_intervention_in_autism_spectrum_disorder_A_meta-analysis_of_randomized_controlled_trials/links/6135760638818c2eaf8574a5/Facial-emotion-training-as-an-intervention-in-autism-spectrum-disorder-A-meta-analysis-of-randomized-controlled-trials.pdf)
- [EMP][WEAK, small] A 12-hour computer program RCT with 32 children reported strong effects on recognizing emotions from real-face photographs and pictograms. — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/evaluation-of-a-new-computer-intervention-to-teach-people-with-autism/)
- [EMP][WEAK, pilot] Computer-based emotion training pilot with preschoolers: gains did not generalize to non-instructional contexts. — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/autism-developmental/a-pilot-study-examining-a-computer-based-intervention-to-improve-recog/)
- [EMP] Ganea, (Bloom) Pickard & DeLoache (2008): 15- and 18-month-olds taught a novel label from picture books with photographs, realistic drawings, or cartoons; more iconic (realistic) pictures produced better learning and better transfer of the label to the real object; cartoons hardest. — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/toddlers-find-photos-easier-learn-drawings); [review table, PMC5807901](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/table/T1); [Nursery World](https://nurseryworld.co.uk/news/article/children-will-learn-more-from-photos)
- [EMP] Simcock & DeLoache: 18–30-month-olds imitated novel actions better from photographs than line drawings. — [review table, PMC5807901](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5807901/table/T1)

### Inferences
- For NUHA's **emotion-recognition** activity: start with simplified/cartoon faces (lower anxiety, more typical processing in autistic children), then systematically progress to real photographs of diverse Saudi/Gulf children and adults (varied age, gender, hijab/ghutra, skin tone, lighting) — generalization is the known failure point, so it must be designed in, not assumed. Include multiple exemplars per emotion; consider a parent "try it in real life" prompt (the Golan group's generalization lesson).
- The Transporters hybrid (stylized body + real face) is a precedent for a mascot style that is still useful for emotion teaching.
- For **environment matching** / object recognition with 2–4-year-olds, avoid highly abstract flat icons for target objects; use realistic or semi-realistic illustrations (or photos) of objects the child will see in Saudi homes/streets. Flat/simplified style is acceptable for UI chrome, buttons, and backgrounds.
- If the target age is 4–10, the photo-advantage evidence (from infants/toddlers) is less directly applicable; stylized-realistic is likely fine.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence found comparing photo vs cartoon vs flat-icon object recognition in autistic children specifically in an app context (only TD toddler data).
- No Arabic/Saudi-specific stimulus validation found (e.g., cultural display-rule differences in emotion faces); NUHA should not assume Western face sets transfer.
- Full text of Rosset et al. (2008) and the RCT meta-analysis not verified; generalization-to-real-photo effect sizes unknown.

## 2. Characters / mascots / pedagogical agents

### Takeaway
Pedagogical agents have a small positive average effect on learning (g ≈ 0.20), larger for K-12 than adults, but there is very little evidence for preschoolers and almost none specifically for autistic or ADHD children in apps. A single, consistent, calm, predictable guide character is defensible; an animated, talkative, always-present character risks becoming a seductive/interactive distraction.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Schroeder, Adesope & Gilbert (2013): 43 studies, 3,088 participants — small but significant learning benefit of pedagogical agents; more beneficial for K-12 than post-secondary learners. No preschool sample. — [karlkapp.com abstract summary](https://karlkapp.com/abstracts-of-three-studies-related-to-pedagogical-agents/); [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/How-Effective-are-Pedagogical-Agents-for/99900601056901842)
- [META] 2021 meta-analysis on multimedia pedagogical agents (testing appearance, motion, voice moderators): small overall effect g+ = 0.20. — [Birmingham research portal](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/effectiveness-of-multimedia-pedagogical-agents-predicted-by-diver/)
- [META][WEAK, abstract only] 2025 Review of Educational Research meta-analysis "Virtual Characters Help K–12 Students Learn and Improve Motivation" — title indicates positive learning and motivation effects in K-12; details not verified. — [metatoc listing](https://metatoc.com/papers/171620-virtual-characters-help-k-12-students-learn-and-improve-motivation-a-meta-analysis)
- [EMP] Hunze et al. (RWTH Aachen): few studies investigate pedagogical agents with young children; little is known about effects on preschoolers; study explores preschoolers' perception of an agent via parasocial-relationship lens. — [RWTH PDF](https://publications.rwth-aachen.de/record/825387/files/825387.pdf)
- [EMP] Golan et al. (2010) Transporters: predictable, mechanical, rule-governed characters were argued to suit autistic children's systemizing preference. — [Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/can-emotion-recognition-be-taught-to-children-with-autism-spectru/)
- [META] Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015): interactive elements (hotspots, games) in e-storybooks distracted children, while multimedia that illustrates the content (animated pictures, music, sound) helped — implying a character is fine when it *carries* content, harmful when it is a side attraction. — [Review of Educational Research PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)

### Inferences
- Use one guide character across all nine activities (consistency/predictability is a core autism-support principle; see section 6). It should appear mainly at instruction and feedback moments, not animate idly during the task.
- The character should demonstrate (model) the task — e.g., trace the letter, tap 1-2-3 — which makes it a prompting tool (see section 4) rather than decoration.
- Keep the character's face simple and its emotional display clear and exaggerated-but-calm; avoid a character whose emotions conflict with the emotion-recognition curriculum.
- Allow caregivers to mute/hide the character's voice/animations.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found on mascots/guide characters in apps for autistic or ADHD children under 10 specifically; recommendation is extrapolated.
- No evidence found on human vs animal vs object (e.g., Transporters vehicles) mascot choice for Saudi children; cultural acceptability (e.g., animal vs human, gender of character) is untested.

## 3. Visual complexity of scenes: decorative vs plain, seductive details

### Takeaway
Strong convergent evidence that irrelevant decoration lowers young children's attention and learning (decorated-classroom study; seductive-details meta-analysis g ≈ −0.33; e-book hotspots distracting). For autistic and ADHD children, sensory-overload concerns push further toward plain, low-clutter, content-only visuals; this part is mostly guideline-level.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Fisher, Godwin & Seltman (2014), *Psychological Science* 25(7): kindergarteners (n=24 in original; sample size not confirmed in retrieved text) in a lab classroom for six science lessons; decorated condition -> more off-task time, more distraction, smaller learning gains than sparse condition. — [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2014/may/may27_decoratedclassrooms.html); [APS release](https://psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/heavily-decorated-classrooms-disrupt-attention-and-learning-in-young-children.html/comment-page-1)
  - Critique: Imuta & Scarf (2014) argue effect may be driven by novelty rather than irrelevance. — [PMC4274873](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4274873/)
  - Follow-up: Godwin et al. (2022), *Cognitive Science* 46(2) e13093, on repeated exposure to the visual environment. — [Learning and the Brain blog](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=6669)
- [META] Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32(3): 58 studies, >7,500 students; seductive details reduce learning, g = −0.33 (secondary-source figure); worse when detail sits next to relevant diagrams or is persistent; authors call the effect fairly small. Mostly older learners. — [WSU news](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [e-teaching summary](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020); [Learning and the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/)
- [META] Takacs, Swart & Bus (2015): 43 studies, 2,147 children; tech-enhanced stories small benefit (comprehension g+ = 0.17; expressive vocab g+ = 0.20); multimedia beneficial, interactive hotspots/games distracting, especially for children from less stimulating homes. — [Leiden repository](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2872126/view); [SAGE PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)
- [GUIDE] Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015), *PSPI* 16(1): apps should be active, engaged (low distraction), meaningful, socially interactive; "fancy features" interrupt cognitive processing. — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/science-of-learning-can-help-parents-developers-grade-educational-apps.html)
- [GUIDE/OPINION] Engineering review of ASD technology (2017, *Applied Sciences*): include only graphics/sound needed for the game goal because of sensory overload risk; make games easy to repeat and advance without delays. — [doi 10.3390/app7101051](https://doi.org/10.3390%2Fapp7101051)
- [GUIDE] French serious-games-for-autism guidelines (Le Mans, EscapeIT project): design around atypical sensory processing; offer parent/teacher customization of visual and audio elements. — [PDF](https://projets-lium.univ-lemans.fr/escapeit/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2019/01/ERP-I1-D3-Bonnes-pratiques-de-conception-de-jeux-sérieux-pour-enfants-autistes.pdf)
- [EMP][WEAK, small qualitative] Padanan Pintar (2024) matching game, co-designed with autistic children: reported calming effect and reduced overstimulation. — [UUM PDF](https://repo.uum.edu.my/32110/1/JCISC%2003%202024%20101-113.pdf)
- [OPINION] Vendor/student examples recommend muted palettes, rounded shapes, subtle animations, predictable mechanics. — [Autism.org.uk directory (SensePlay)](https://www.autism.org.uk/autism-services-directory/digital-health-hub-ltd); [UCL student project](https://students.cs.ucl.ac.uk/2024/group6/ui-design.html)

### Inferences
- NUHA activity screens: plain or very low-detail background (soft single color/gentle gradient), the task objects as the only high-contrast elements; no idle animations, no decorative characters in the play area during the task.
- Path tracing, star tracking, ball maze, number tapping are attention tasks — any decoration directly competes with the target; keep path/target contrast high and distractors absent unless distraction is the deliberate difficulty variable (graded, opt-in).
- Novelty critique suggests decoration may matter less once familiar — but do not rely on that; keep consistent layouts across sessions.
- Provide a caregiver "calm mode" setting (reduced color saturation, no motion, sound off).

### Gaps
- No direct experimental study found on decorative backgrounds in tablet apps for autistic/ADHD children; evidence is from TD kindergarteners and older learners, plus guidelines.

## 4. Feedback design: immediate vs delayed, informative vs praise, errors without distress, prompting hierarchies

### Takeaway
For this population, the strongest-evidence approach is ABA-style: immediate, consistent, specific feedback; prevent errors via prompting (most-to-least or prompt delay) and fade prompts; respond to errors neutrally with a correction/hint rather than negative signals. Most-to-least reduces errors but can slow learning; time-delay variants combine low errors with speed.

### Cited Findings
- [GUIDE/META] NCAEP 2020 report (Steinbrenner et al.): systematic review of 1990–2017 literature; 28 evidence-based practices for autistic children/youth, including Antecedent-Based Interventions, Differential Reinforcement, Naturalistic Intervention (and, per the full report, Prompting, Reinforcement, Time Delay, Visual Supports, Technology-Aided Instruction — not verified in retrieved snippet). — [NCAEP report page](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/?p=395); [Executive summary PDF](https://ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/sites/ncaep.fpg.unc.edu/files/imce/documents/EBP%20Executive%20Summary.pdf)
  - Critique: Leaf et al. (2021) argue NCAEP inclusion criteria were loose. — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/the-evidence-based-practices-for-children--youth--and-young-adults-wit/)
- [EMP, single-case] Libby et al. (2008), *Behavior Analysis in Practice* 1(1): children with autism building Lego structures; MTL (most-to-least) produced fewer errors than LTM (least-to-most), all learned with MTL, but 3 participants learned faster with LTM; MTL with delay (MTLD) was nearly as fast as LTM with fewer errors. — [PMC2846579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2846579)
- [EMP, single-case, n=3] Cengher et al. (2015): MTL more effective and efficient than LTM for all 3 participants; authors note comparisons across studies are variable. — [PMC4893031](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4893031)
- [EMP, single-case, n=3] Conn et al. (2021): responsive prompt delay at least as effective as MTL and "no-no" prompting. — [Bangor](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/a-comparison-of-most-to-least-prompting-no-no-prompting-and-respo/)
- [REVIEW] Demchak (1990), cited by Libby: MTL associated with fewer errors than LTM for chaining; constant time delay as effective as LTM and more efficient. — [PMC2846579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2846579)
- [GUIDE] Practitioner sources: MTL commonly paired with errorless learning to reduce errors. — [SWSC PDF](https://www.swsc.org/cms/lib/MN01000693/Centricity/Domain/76/LTM_MTL_Prompting.pdf); [Autism Classroom Resources](https://autismclassroomresources.com/?p=636)
- [EMP] ADHD and reinforcement: Luman et al. (2009) compared ADHD vs autism vs TD on reward frequency vs magnitude in reinforcement learning; children with ADHD less accurate during learning, particularly in 100% and 85% reward-probability conditions. — [UvA DARE](https://dare.uva.nl/id/ba1787cb-203c-4196-9316-5c8538002cbc)
- [EMP] Rosch & Hawk (2013): performance-contingent rewards enhanced flanker P3 similarly in ADHD and controls, suggesting rewards may help cognitive performance in ADHD. — [PMC3807761](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3807761)
- [EMP] Delay aversion in ADHD: study of 360 ADHD probands, 349 siblings, 112 controls supports a dual model (immediate-reward drive + delay aversion); effect larger when choosing small-soon reduced trial delay. Experienced (real-time) waiting, not hypothetical, drives steeper discounting in 7–12-year-olds with ADHD. — [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/66171); [SAGE 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0081246320964350)
- [GUIDE/OPINION] Autism game guidance: immediate and consistent feedback; configurable challenge; easy repetition without delay. — [SBGames LetraMania](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbgames_estendido/article/download/45906/45667/); [doi 10.3390/app7101051](https://doi.org/10.3390%2Fapp7101051)

### Inferences (UI translation)
- **Prompt hierarchy as UI** (per activity): (1) full model — guide character demonstrates / animated ghost hand traces the path or letter; (2) partial — highlight/glow on the correct target or start dot of the stroke; (3) minimal — subtle pulse; (4) independent. Fade by success streak (MTL), or use *prompt delay*: give the child ~3–5 s (configurable) before the hint appears (MTLD / time delay — the best speed/error tradeoff in Libby).
- **Errors**: no red X, buzzer, sad face, or lost points. Neutral response: the wrong item gently returns/doesn't accept; immediately offer the next prompt level; the trial counts as "prompted correct". For letter writing/tracing, constrain strokes (snap to guide) at early levels = errorless.
- **Immediate feedback** (<~0.5 s) for every response, especially for ADHD (delay aversion; experienced waiting is aversive). Avoid long celebration animations that create waiting before the next trial.
- **Informative > generic**: feedback should say what was right ("٣ بعد ٢ — أحسنت") rather than only "ممتاز". (Informational vs controlling distinction from SDT; see section 5.)
- Log prompt level per trial so caregivers/therapists see progress from prompted to independent.

### Gaps
- Primary Luman, Oosterlaan & Sergeant (2005) review on reinforcement contingencies in ADHD (Clin Psych Rev 25(2)) not retrieved; claims that ADHD benefits most from frequent/immediate reinforcement are commonly cited but not verified here.
- No study found that directly tests prompting hierarchies implemented as app UI (vs therapist-delivered) for autistic children.
- No evidence found on audio vs visual error feedback distress specifically.

## 5. Rewards: stars, points, badges, celebrations; overjustification; token economies; ADHD and flashy celebrations

### Takeaway
Rewards are double-edged. Tangible/expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation in general populations (Deci et al. 1999), while positive, informational verbal feedback enhances it; this is contested (Cameron & Pierce). In autism/ID, token economies are a long-standing, well-supported technology (mostly narrative reviews and single-case designs). Practical synthesis: use a simple, predictable token system (e.g., fill 5 stars -> short preferred activity) with informational praise, keep celebrations brief and low-intensity, and allow caregiver adjustment. No direct evidence found on flashy celebration effects in ADHD.

### Cited Findings
- [META] Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), *Psychological Bulletin* 125: 128 experiments; engagement-, completion-, and performance-contingent rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); all tangible and all expected rewards also undermined; positive feedback (verbal rewards) enhanced free-choice behavior and self-reported interest. — [UW PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [UBalt PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
  - Note: the widely cited child-specific breakdown (verbal rewards enhanced for college students but not children; tangible effects stronger for children) could not be confirmed from retrieved text — see Gaps.
- [META, contested] Cameron & Pierce (1994) concluded negative reward effects are limited and easily prevented; Deci et al. (2001) called that analysis flawed; Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) rebutted ("the myth continues"). — [Deci et al. 2001 PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf); [Cameron et al. 2001](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/9460423c-1ed9-4760-995d-5dd57e6628aa)
- [REVIEW] Matson & Boisjoli (2009), *Research in Developmental Disabilities*: token economy is "one of the most important technologies" of ABA over 40 years for children with ID/autism; narrative review, not meta-analysis. — [LSU repository](https://repository.lsu.edu/psychology_pubs/1372)
- [REVIEW] NASET literature review: token economies increase attending, on-task, and responding, decrease challenging behavior in autistic students; one study found no difference vs primary reinforcement (satiation not controlled). — [NASET](https://www.naset.com/publications/autism-spectrum-disorders-series/exploring-the-use-of-token-economies-for-students-on-the-autism-spectrum-a-brief-review-of-the-literature)
- [REVIEW, older] Kazdin & Bootzin: token economy obstacles include non-responsiveness and poor generalization of gains. — [Arizona experts](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-token-economy-an-evaluative-review/)
- [EMP] Carnett et al. (2014) (cited in thesis): research on pairing tokens with praise/attention, response cost, and reinforcement schedules within token economies. — [UGA thesis](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/9708/files/efird_lydia_e_201908_ms.pdf)
- [EMP] Rewards improved cognitive (flanker) performance markers similarly in ADHD and controls. — [PMC3807761](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3807761)
- [EMP] Children with ADHD collected small immediately available rewards more often than TD children (136 children, 90 ADHD, 2022). — [Mount Sinai scholars](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/waiting-and-working-for-rewards-attention-deficithyperactivity-di/)
- [META] Interactive game add-ons in e-books distracted young children (relevant to "reward mini-games" between trials). — [SAGE PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989)
- [GUIDE] Sensory overload caution: only include graphics/sound needed for the game goal. — [doi 10.3390/app7101051](https://doi.org/10.3390%2Fapp7101051)

### Inferences
- **Token board, not points economy**: a visible strip of 3–5 empty star slots per session block; each correct (or prompted-correct) trial fills one; when full -> short "free choice" reward (e.g., 20–30 s of a calm sensory animation, bubbles, or favorite item chosen by child/caregiver). This mirrors a clinical token board, which autistic children and therapists in Saudi centers likely already know.
- **Celebration intensity**: brief (≤1–1.5 s), gentle (soft chime, small sparkle around the target, character nod) per trial; one slightly bigger celebration at token-board completion. No screen-filling confetti/flashing every trial; flashing also has photosensitivity considerations. Make celebration intensity a caregiver setting (off / gentle / lively) because some ADHD children may need more salient feedback while sensory-sensitive autistic children need less.
- **Avoid**: leaderboards, loss/streak-break punishments, lives, timers that penalize, unlockable shops that become the goal — these are controlling, expected, tangible-like rewards with the strongest undermining evidence and add competing interactive elements.
- Pair every token with specific verbal (Arabic) praise — informational feedback is the reward type that enhances intrinsic motivation; plan to thin the token schedule as mastery rises.

### Gaps
- Child vs adult moderator values in Deci et al. (1999) not verified (PDF text not extractable here).
- No empirical study found on celebration-animation intensity in apps for ADHD or autistic children; recommendation is inference.
- No meta-analysis of gamification specifically for young children with autism/ADHD retrieved; an unverified secondary claim of a 2022 pooled analysis of 24 elementary token-economy studies with large gains was not confirmed.

## 6. Progress visualization: visual schedules, first-then boards, timers (TEACCH/visual supports) and UI translation

### Takeaway
Visual activity schedules are an established evidence-based practice for autism (31 studies, 16 acceptable quality, preschool to adult), especially combined with systematic instruction; visual supports are an NCAEP EBP. Translate as: session schedule strip ("first-then"), within-activity progress indicator showing how much is left, and predictable start/end signals.

### Cited Findings
- [META/SR] Knight, Sartini & Spriggs (2015), *JADD* 45(1): reviewed 1993–2013; 31 studies met criteria, 16 acceptable quality; visual activity schedules are an EBP for people with ASD, especially with systematic instructional procedures; can increase, maintain and generalize skills preschool through adulthood. — [PDF](https://cbmt.franciscan.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2021/07/Knight-et-al.-2015_Visual-Schedules.pdf)
- [SR, thesis][WEAK] 2020 UGA thesis review coding VAS type and technology use; reports high-quality evidence of positive VAS effects. — [UGA thesis](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/5610/files/chatlen_emily_v_202012_ms.pdf)

### Inferences (UI translation)
- **Session schedule**: horizontal strip of 2–4 activity icons for today (caregiver-set); current one highlighted; completed ones checked/greyed. Arabic RTL: the strip should run right-to-left.
- **First-then**: "أولاً [activity] ثم [reward/break]" card before each activity, using the same icons each time.
- **Within-activity progress**: fixed number of trials shown as dots/stars (doubles as token board) so the child knows when it ends; avoid indefinite play.
- **Timers**: if used, show a visual shrinking disc (Time-Timer style) for breaks, not as pressure on task performance; no penalty on timeout.
- **Transitions**: consistent ending ritual (character waves, "انتهينا") and a 1-screen warning before switching activity.
- Use the same pictogram style for schedule icons as the activity entry tiles (consistency).
